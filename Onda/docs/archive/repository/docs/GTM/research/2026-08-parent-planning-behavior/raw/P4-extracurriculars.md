# P4 — Year-Round Extracurriculars + Youth Sports (the weekly logistics grind)

Collector P4. US only. Compiled 2026-08-21. Brief: `../BRIEF.md`.

Already known per BRIEF, not re-derived: youth sports $1,016/child (Aspen 2025), extracurriculars
$731/child, US camps ~$4.6B. This report goes past all three.

### Interpretation

**1. Logistics now outranks money as the stated pain — and that is a 2026 finding, not a 2019 one.**
The single most decision-relevant number in this report is #1: in a probability-panel poll fielded five
weeks ago, ~8 in 10 youth-sports parents named scheduling/logistics/time as a challenge, above every
cost item at ~7 in 10. Every prior narrative about youth sports (and the BRIEF's own "already known"
list) is a *cost* narrative. The cost story is real but it is a *sorting* story — it decides who plays
(#20, #21, #23). The logistics story is a *daily* story, and it is the one that afflicts the exact
households that can pay for software (#24: the $100K+ cohort drives more, volunteers more, and carries
more sports-day minutes than the poorest cohort — 30.3 vs 23.3 minutes of driving, 4.91 vs 3.36
volunteer hours). Willingness-to-pay and pain are, unusually, aligned here.

**2. The coordination slice is bigger than the driving slice, and it is entirely message-shaped.**
Finding #3/#4 is the sharpest product signal in the domain. On a sports day the parent spends 27.6
minutes driving — but 37.0 minutes communicating (20.1 with other team parents, 16.9 with coaches) and
another 30.3 minutes on equipment logistics. Driving cannot be automated by an app; *37 minutes a day
of inbound-message triage across a group chat, a team app, and a coach's email* is exactly what an
ingestion-plus-summarize product removes. Note the shape: this is **inbound comms handling**, not
outbound planning. The product's Gmail ingestion and photo/flyer ingestion are aimed at the right
organ; the Planner's 12-knob discovery agent is aimed at a once-a-season problem (#8: 47.8% of children
re-register every 1–4 months, so discovery fires maybe 4–6 times a year per family) while comms fires
daily.

**3. The task family is fundamentally many-to-many, and every existing tool is one-to-one.**
Stack the multipliers: 1.58 participating children per household (#25) × 2.01 sports each (#6) × 1.86
organizational settings for the primary sport alone (#9), plus 44.5% of kids in a club and 41.0% in
music/dance/arts lessons (#13) which are different providers again. There is no plausible reading of
these numbers in which a family's activity life lives in fewer than three or four separate systems, and
the median is higher. The one direct measurement (#31) puts 44% of parents at 7–9 apps per child and
only 21% with any unified view — n=125 and vendor-sponsored, so directional, but it points the same way
as the structural arithmetic. **No incumbent owns the cross-activity, cross-kid view, and the vendors
say so themselves** (#34): TeamSnap bills per team rather than per account, its "all-team" view only
spans teams inside TeamSnap, it ships an account-merge tool because parents accumulate multiple logins,
and SportsEngine's official answer to a two-household family is *"we suggest the parents get a shared
free email account."* That is not a product gap the incumbents are about to close — it is their billing
model. Note also (#33) that these apps are genuinely large — GameChanger alone books ~$150M/yr from
~10M users, per an audited SEC filing — so the opening is emphatically **not** "build a better team
app." It is the layer above them.

**4. The "extracurricular" vertical being feature-flagged OFF is the largest misallocation this
research surfaces.** Camp (on by default) is a 1–2 episode/year job. Extracurriculars are a
150–250-event/year job (scorecard row 2) affecting 72.7% of children 6–17 (#12) versus camp's much
smaller reach, and they are the job parents themselves name as their top challenge (#1). The vertical
flags are inverted relative to demand.

**5. Two contradictions worth naming.**
(a) **Participation is measured two incompatible ways** (#22): NSCH's parent-reported "on a team or
took lessons in the past 12 months" gives 55.4%, SFIA's "played at least once" gives 65%, and Project
Play's own "regular participation" measure gives figures in the high 30s/low 40s. Anyone quoting a
single youth-sports participation rate is quoting one of three different constructs. For sizing a
*logistics* product the relevant denominator is the regular/in-season one, which is smaller than the
headline — but the relevant denominator for *all* organized activity is NSCH's 72.7% (#12), which is
larger than any sports number.
(b) **Cost as a barrier vs. cost as a driver.** AA3PM says 56% of non-enrollers cite cost (#18) while
65% cite "my child prefers other activities" (#19) — and AP-Ipsos finds high- and low-income sports
parents report *similar* annual spend (#21), contradicting the Aspen/AA3PM picture of a 9× spending
gap (#17). The reconciliation is that AA3PM measures spend across **all** out-of-school activities and
AP-Ipsos measures **sports only among families already playing**: the gap is in *breadth of activities*,
not in the price of any one of them. That distinction matters — the affluent family's pain is
*coordinating five things*, not *affording one*.

**6. The time burden is what makes kids quit, which makes it a retention story too.** #14's gradient
(19.9% → 40.3% → 55.3%) is unusually clean: perceived time burden is the variable that separates
current from former from never athletes. A tool that reduces the family's felt schedule load is
plausibly upstream of participation itself — a positioning claim that is defensible from Tier B data.

**7. The federal number and the survey number look 3× apart and are not actually in conflict — but
only one of them sizes the product.** ATUS says child-transport is 11.4 minutes a day (#27); Aspen
says 27.6 minutes of sports driving on a sports day (#3), and NHTS says ~4.4 min/day of escort travel
(#29). All three are right and all three are averages over the wrong denominator. The reconciling
number is #28, computed from ATUS microdata for this report: **on any given day only 28% of adults in
households with a child under 18 do any child-transport at all — and for that 28% it is 44 minutes.**
This is a *concentrated, episodic* burden, not a smooth one, which is precisely the shape that makes
scheduling software valuable (the pain is spiky and calendar-triggered) and precisely the shape that
makes headline daily averages useless for sizing it. Note also that ATUS 180301 and NHTS
serve-passenger are **biased downward in opposite ways** and must never be added: ATUS excludes escort
legs logged under other travel purposes; NHTS chains escort legs into the destination's purpose.

**8. The gender split is stable across every instrument.** ATUS: women 0.22 vs men 0.16 hr/day of
child-transport (#27), 33.5% vs 21.3% daily participation (#28), and 0.16 vs 0.04 hr/day on
education-related child activities — a 4× gap (#30). Aspen's youth-sports parent sample is 58.6%
female (#25). This corroborates the BRIEF's existing mental-load material from an independent federal
source, and it means the buyer for this vertical is disproportionately the mother in a
dual-parent household.

**9. Cohort reading.** Every intensity number in this report describes a Millennial household: mean
parent age 40.84, kids averaging 11.59 (#25). Gen X parents own the tail — the 17.3% travel/club
segment (#23), the 20.5% of kids playing 9–12 months a year (#8), the 13–17 band where regular
participation is falling but survivors are most committed. Gen Z parents (18–29) are almost entirely
parents of under-6s, an age band the federal instrument does not even ask about; their load in this
task family is near zero and will remain so for 3–8 years. **Building extracurricular logistics is
building for Millennials and Gen X. It is not a Gen Z acquisition surface.**

## Section 2: WEAK SIGNAL (not sourced to Tier A/B/C)

- **Enrollment-episode duration.** No source anywhere measures how long a US activity registration
  takes a parent — minutes, form fields, or number of portals. Genuine gap; worth primary research.
- **Registration seasonality and sell-outs.** No national dataset. Observable pattern from primary
  municipal sources only: fall youth-sports registration typically **opens May–June and closes
  mid-July–mid-August** (e.g. Minneapolis Park & Rec opened Fall 2025 registration May 21 2025;
  Columbia County GA ran Jun 29–Jul 31; Forney TX closed Jul 24 with a $25 late fee Jul 25–26; Lincoln
  NE deadlines Jul 7–Aug 10). These are real primary documents but a convenience sample of four, not a
  survey. **Sell-out evidence is negative where it exists** — Minneapolis explicitly guarantees a team
  placement to everyone who registers. Treat "activities sell out" as unproven for rec sports; it may
  hold for premium/specialty programs but I found no data.
- **"70% of kids drop out of sports by 13."** Circulates everywhere; a Tucson.com column
  ("70% of kids drop out of sports by 13? Why the ubiquitous stat is wrong") traces it to no
  defensible origin. **Treat as laundered — do not use.** The defensible replacements are #14 above
  and Project Play's "kids quit most sports by age 11 / average child plays under three years,"
  itself from a **2019** Aspen–Utah State survey and now dated.
- **LendingTree "79% of parents went into debt for kids' competitive activities."** Press release,
  n>1,000, method thinly disclosed, vendor-interested, and irreconcilable with the Harris Poll figure
  of **18%** going into debt for sports. A 4× discrepancy between two vendor polls means neither is
  usable. Both go here, not in Section 1.
- **CNBC 2022 "nearly 60% of families say youth sports are a financial strain."** Dated and the
  underlying instrument was not located.
- **App-sprawl count — no probability-sampled study exists.** Checked in their primary documents and
  **none of them asks how many apps, portals or logins a family manages**: National Parents Union
  (2026 waves, n=1,527 May 28–Jun 1 2026 ±2.7 and n=1,511 Feb 12–18 2026 ±2.6 — edtech appears only via
  AI and data privacy), Learning Heroes, EdWeek Research Center, Speak Up / Project Tomorrow, Pew,
  Common Sense Media, Gallup/Walton, and ParentSquare's own "State of School-Home Communication"
  (n=1,600+ **educators**, 2026 — a qualitative consolidation theme, no tool count). Finding #31 is the
  only parent-level number in existence and it is n=125, vendor-partnered, non-probability, with a
  screener that selects for parents already using 3+ apps. **This is a genuine, fundable gap: a clean
  probability-panel question "how many separate apps or portals do you use for your children's
  activities?" does not exist and would be a proprietary asset.**
- **Vendor scale claims I could NOT independently verify — do not cite from this report.** TeamSnap
  "30M+ users / 19,000+ orgs" (and the 25M figure at the 2021 Waud Capital investment), SportsEngine's
  "more than 30 million" (which appears to be NBC Sports Next *combined* boilerplate rather than a
  SportsEngine-only figure), LeagueApps "3,000+ orgs / 10M+ kids", PlayMetrics "2,700+ orgs", Band,
  Sawyer, ClassDojo, ParentSquare and Brightwheel numbers. These reached me second-hand and I ran out
  of search budget before confirming them at origin. All are self-reported, unaudited, and define
  "user" however they like. **Finding #33 (GameChanger, SEC-filed) is the only scale number in this
  category I stand behind.** Same treatment for app-store rating and review counts (e.g. "GameChanger
  4.88★/879,274 reviews") and for any verbatim app-store review text — unverified, excluded.
- **A correction to a claim of laundering.** I was told "85% of parents rate their multi-app setup
  ≤5/10" was a misquote of Cornerstone's 42%. Reading the source PDF myself: Cornerstone reports 12%
  at 2, 18% at 3, 13% at 4 and **42% at 5** — which sums to **85% at 5 or below**. The 85% figure is
  arithmetically correct, just never stated that way in the report. Usable with that explanation; the
  42% and the 85% are different statistics, not a contradiction.
- **Genuinely laundered app-sprawl derivatives — do not use.**
  "Districts use 3–7 apps parents must download" has no linked study and appears fabricated around
  Cornerstone's response buckets. "Parents get updates through 5–6 different apps" (solvedconsulting)
  is uncited prose. "60% of parents miss important events" is attributed to Cornerstone and **is not in
  the Cornerstone PDF**.
- **TeamSnap "24 million users."** No origin. The company's own PRs say 25M (2021) and 30M (2026).
  Aggregator artifact — cite the PRs or nothing. Band's US youth-sports figures ("5–6M US MAU",
  "65% schools and sports teams") appear only in secondary outlets with no Naver newsroom origin.
- **Vendor scale numbers that simply do not exist publicly**: SportsEngine-only user counts (the "30
  million" is NBC Sports Next combined boilerplate), SportsEngine Play subscribers, Sawyer provider and
  activity counts, Jackrabbit Class customers, DaySmart Recreation customers, PlayMetrics pre-Genstar
  funding, Brightwheel's current center count. Also note a factual correction to a commonly repeated
  claim: **Blue Sombrero is owned by Stack Sports, not NBC Sports Next**, and **Bloomz was not acquired
  by ParentSquare** (ParentSquare acquired Remind, Nov 2023); Bloomz's own "26,000 schools" traces to a
  ~2016 blog post and is stale.
- **Method trap to avoid**: Instructure/LearnPlatform's 2,982-tools-per-district figure (#35) measures
  *institutional* procurement, not parent-side apps. Equating the two is exactly the laundering move
  this brief warns about. Its 9th edition (2026) also changed methodology to Canvas-LTI-only, so no
  trend line may cross 2024-25.
- **Anything cut by parent generation.** Not one source in this domain reports by parent birth cohort.
  Kid-age is used throughout as the proxy. Stated as a limitation, not smuggled in as a finding.
