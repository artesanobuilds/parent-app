# Idea Roast: MVP — a $199/yr AI family chief-of-staff for parents raising kids across two households (camp/activity discovery, flyer/email→calendar ingestion, cross-household conflict detection, AI coach retention)

Run 2026-07-07 via the 5-persona adversarial council (Contrarian, Expansionist, First-Principles, Deep Researcher, Buyer) + independent Judge. Full council transcripts in the Appendix.

## VERDICT: RESHAPE  (confidence: high)

The pain is real, the buyer is trained to pay, and the product is already built — but the evidence dismantles all four pillars of the *as-specified* plan: the differentiating two-household features require the estranged co-parent's active adoption (the one user with a disincentive to join); $199/yr is priced above every surviving AI family assistant (Ohai $99, Nori ~$96, Skylight $79) and the only $200+/yr spend in the category buys court-admissible documentation, not logistics; the coach-as-retention thesis is contradicted by the buyer himself (he'd pay *more* with the coach removed — coach transcripts are subpoena bait in custody disputes); and the camp wedge is out of season for the exact 90-day window chosen to validate. Meanwhile two well-funded versions of the full "AI family chief-of-staff" bundle died within the last 16 months (Milo, Yohana) while the narrow email/flyer→calendar slice thrives. The engine is right; the packaging, price, retention story, and launch timing are wrong.

## The reshaped version

**Keep (the engine):**
- The ingestion plumbing — flyer photo / forwarded email → tracked activity + calendar + typed to-dos. This is the demo magic, the proven-paid category (Skylight: 1.3M families, 99% growth), and it's already built.
- The discovery agent + coverage-gap detection — the coverage-gap screen ("June 15–19: neither home has coverage, here are 4 camps with openings for a 10-year-old") is the single feature the Buyer said would convert him on the spot.
- The persistent family profile and the two-voice founder content plan.

**Kill (or demote):**
- **The two-player requirement as the promise.** Sell the single-player product honestly: "the organized parent's chief of staff." Parent #2 participates at ZERO friction — accepts plain Google Calendar invites and forwards emails to a magic address; never installs an app, never creates an account, never pays. (The Buyer, roleplaying his ex: "she would NOT install an app because I asked… but she would absolutely forward a camp confirmation email to a magic address.") Cross-household gap detection bootstraps from the buyer entering the custody pattern themselves.
- **$199/yr as the anchor.** Test $99–$149/family/yr in the WTP question (buckets centered lower). The $199-vs-OFW comparison is dishonest for court-ordered buyers — MVP is *additive* to their $432, not a replacement.
- **The coach as the retention/marketing pillar.** Retention job moves to the always-on ingestion loop (school-email tsunami + fall/spring registration + camps — the Activity OS relay). The coach stays in the product but needs a privacy answer FIRST for this segment (no-store/export/delete guarantees, discoverability-aware design) — for co-parents it's currently a liability surface, not a hook.
- **Camp-planning as the July launch message.** It's out of season until January. Launch into what's next on the calendar: fall activity registration (Aug–Sep) + back-to-school email chaos.

**Aim at instead:** solo-first chief-of-staff for the load-bearing parent in a two-household family, at ~$129/yr, entered via fall-registration/school-email pain, with a written data-trust kit (export anytime, deletion, 90-day sunset promise, coach-privacy stance) as a first-class feature — because for this buyer, data policy IS product.

## The three findings that matter most

1. **Second-parent activation is the structural flaw — and the Buyer handed over the fix.** Three personas independently converged on it (Contrarian's #1 fatal flaw, First-Principles' weakest link, the Buyer's first objection). OurFamilyWizard only solved it with court mandates. The fix is product design, not marketing: solo-first value + participation-without-adoption for the ex (calendar invites + email forwarding, no account). This converts the funnel's choke point from "convince my adversary to install an app" to "my adversary keeps doing what she already does."
2. **The pricing evidence contradicts $199.** The Researcher's table is unambiguous: surviving AI family assistants cluster at $39–99/yr; Milo died at $40/mo; the only $200+ spend is court-mandated documentation. The Buyer's fair zone: $99–149, and "charge ME once, make it genuinely free for her." The GTM research's $199 hypothesis anchored against OFW's per-parent pricing — but court-ordered buyers can't cancel OFW, so MVP competes with the $39–99 organizers for incremental wallet, not with OFW.
3. **The coach-as-retention thesis fails three separate ways.** First-Principles: if the coach were strong enough to retain it would be strong enough to be the wedge. Contrarian: free general AI substitutes at churn-decision quality. Buyer (the kill shot): coach transcripts about co-parenting stress are discoverable in custody litigation — "I will never type an honest word into it… I'd pay more for the product with the coach *removed*." Retention must come from the always-on ingestion loop; the coach needs a privacy architecture before it's marketable to this segment.

## The cheapest test — next 48 hours

**Falsify the second-parent-participation assumption for <$50:** message 25 separated/divorced parents (r/Coparenting, r/Daddit, r/singledads, co-parenting Facebook groups, personal network) with the solo-mode pitch — "an app for YOU that turns camp emails/flyers into your calendar and flags weeks where neither home has coverage; your ex never installs anything, just accepts calendar invites / forwards emails" — and ask two questions: (a) would you pay ~$10/mo for this, (b) would your ex realistically accept invites/forward emails to a shared address? **Pass = ≥5 of 25 say they'd pay AND ≥15 of 25 say the zero-friction ex participation would actually happen.** Fail on (b) means even the reshaped version collapses into a single-household organizer competing with free — reposition to the maxed-out-mom persona before spending anything on ads.

## What the council missed

**Slot scarcity is the real "Hunger Games," and it points at the killer feature nobody scored.** The Researcher grazed it — the sharpest camp pain is *getting a spot* (registration filling in 3 minutes), which a discovery/planning agent doesn't solve; a recommendation list of full camps is a taunt. But no persona followed the product implication: **registration-window tracking** — "Camp HBG registration opens Jan 14 at 9am, here's your reminder + prefilled details" — turns the agent from a list-maker into a spot-getter, is uniquely enabled by the discovery agent + family profile already built, and is a wow no incumbent (Skylight, Ohai, ActivityHero) delivers. It also naturally re-arms the product every season, which is the retention answer the coach was failing to provide.

# Appendix — full council transcripts

## A. Contrarian (3/10)

### Steelman (2 sentences)

Divorced co-parents are a rare consumer segment already trained to pay $150–300/yr per parent for logistics software, and this product attacks a genuinely miserable, high-emotion job (camp season across two households) with a founder who is literally the customer and a working product that cost almost nothing to build. If the AI research agent reliably turns "camp panic" into a solved calendar, the $199 price is trivially justified against the cost of one week of scramble childcare, and the persistent family profile could compound into real switching costs.

### 1. Unit economics: CAC ≥ LTV, and the math is not close enough to fix with optimization

**CAC estimate.** They have no audience, so acquisition is paid or organic-from-zero. Divorce-adjacent and parenting keywords are among the most expensive in consumer search ("co-parenting app" is bid on by OFW, TalkingParents, AppClose; divorce-adjacent CPCs run $4–10). Assume a generous $4 CPC, 6% landing-to-trial (hard paywall, unknown brand, Gmail permission ask), 30% trial-to-paid. CAC = 4 / (0.06 × 0.30) ≈ **$220**. Meta lookalikes on "divorced parents" are a sensitive-category targeting minefield post-privacy changes; I'd expect worse there, not better.

**LTV estimate.** $199/yr, minus 15–30% Apple tax on iOS subscriptions, minus AI COGS (~$0.64/discovery run sounds cheap until an engaged family runs discovery, coach chats, and Gmail parsing continuously — call it $10–25/yr/family), minus support. Net year-one contribution ≈ $140–160. Renewal for a seasonal-panic purchase I'd put at 30–40%, giving expected lifetime ~1.5 years → **net LTV ≈ $210–240**.

**LTV:CAC ≈ 1.0–1.1.** That is a business that converts ad dollars into revenue at par, before payroll. And the practical version is worse: $20/day = $1,800 over the 90-day test = **~8 paying customers** at this CAC. That is not a demand signal; that's noise. The test as designed cannot succeed *or* fail informatively on paid.

### 2. Churn structure: three stacked churn mechanisms

- **Seasonality.** Camp panic is a 10–12 week window (roughly late January–April). The wedge produces customers whose acute problem is *fully resolved by June*. Renewal lands 9 months after the last moment of felt pain. This is the gym-in-January dynamic with a 12-month billing cycle — cancel-at-renewal is the default behavior, and the "retention: the coach" thesis is completely unproven (zero evidence anyone retains for an AI parenting coach when ChatGPT is free and already knows your kids if you tell it).
- **"Solved it, cancel."** The best version of the product is self-defeating: if the agent nails camp planning in March, the user has extracted ~90% of annual value in month one of a 12-month prepay. Year two, they know the drill and the panic is smaller.
- **Timing kill-shot for the 90-day test:** it is July. The camp wedge is *dead until January*. Their entire GTM hook is out of season for the exact window in which they demanded a demand signal. Fall activities are a weaker, more fragmented panic.

### 3. The two-sided adoption paradox (this is the structural fatal flaw)

The differentiated features — cross-household conflict detection, coverage gaps *across households*, "one price covers both parents" — require **the ex to onboard, connect their calendar, and grant Gmail access to an app their former spouse chose and paid for.** The target segment is *defined by* coordination failure and, frequently, active distrust. The people who most need cross-household sync are precisely the people least able to agree to install the same app. OurFamilyWizard solved this with the only mechanism that works: **judges order both parties to use it.** This startup has no court channel, no attorney referral network, and no time to build one. Without the second parent, the product degrades into a single-household family organizer — a crowded, cheap category (Cozi is free, Skylight is $150 one-time hardware) — and the co-parent positioning becomes a liability rather than a wedge. Also note "one price covers both parents" is a payment landmine: *which* parent pays for the ex's software is itself a co-parenting dispute.

### 4. Free substitutes and platform absorption

- **Today, free:** shared Google Calendar + ChatGPT (which accepts flyer photos, plans camps, drafts schedules, and remembers your family via its memory feature, at $0–20/mo for *everything*, not just parenting) + the town Facebook group / ActivityHero / Sawyer for camp discovery. A motivated $90K+ HHI parent is already doing exactly this.
- **This quarter, platform-native:** Gmail has auto-extracted events into Calendar for a decade; Gemini in Workspace and Apple Intelligence are actively shipping "photo/email → calendar entry → reminder" as free OS features. The flyer-to-calendar pipeline — the demo-magic core — is being commoditized by the two companies that own the calendar and the inbox this product depends on. You cannot out-integrate Google with Google's own APIs.

### 5. Moat: the founder's own build story is the copycat proof

One engineer, nights and weekends, ~2 months, AI-augmented. That *is* the moat analysis. The stack is prompts + scaffolding over a frontier model + public APIs; a copycat replicates it in weeks. OFW (~1M users, court-mandated distribution, decades of trust) can bolt an AI concierge onto its existing paying base as a feature announcement; Skylight already owns the kitchen-counter family-calendar slot with hardware and a large ad budget and has been shipping AI chore/meal features. The only durable asset — the persistent family profile — is thin at month one and portable in the age of "export my data and paste it into the next tool."

### 6. Distribution: zero audience × 10 hrs/week × out-of-season wedge

The realistic channels for this category are (a) court/attorney/mediator referrals — a multi-year BD grind they have no hours for; (b) SEO/content trust-building — a 12–18 month compounding play starting from zero; (c) mom-community virality — but co-parenting is a *shame-adjacent, low-shareability* topic; people don't post "check out my divorce logistics app" in the class WhatsApp. Paid is CAC-broken per §1. App Store search for "co-parenting" is owned by incumbents with tens of thousands of reviews. Two day-job founders at 10 hrs/week combined-marketing cannot execute any channel that actually works here.

### 7. Trust and regulatory: the ask is maximal, the brand is zero

An unknown, two-person, no-entity-visible app asks for: Gmail read access, both parents' calendars, children's names/ages/schedules, and de facto custody-arrangement data. Three specific problems:

- **Gmail restricted scopes require Google's CASA security verification** — their own project notes flag this as long-lead. Until it clears, the flagship email-ingestion feature can't ship broadly. The wedge feature is regulatorily gated at launch.
- **Custody data is litigation data.** Cross-household schedule records, missed-pickup logs, and AI coach transcripts are subpoena bait in custody disputes. The first news story titled "her ex's AI app was used against her in family court" ends the company. Incumbents have legal frameworks and court legitimacy for exactly this; a garage app does not.
- **AI parenting coach + high-conflict families + crisis scenarios** is a liability surface they clearly already understand (they built crisis-lock infrastructure and shelved the kid voice companions for "regulatory caution") — which tells you the founders themselves know the risk profile of AI-near-children products. The coach is the retention thesis *and* the liability epicenter.

### 8. Founder-fit

Founder-as-customer is real and cuts both ways: he has built the product *he* wants, validated on friend-families who cannot say no. Solo engineer = total bus factor on a system with Railway + DuckDB + LangGraph + APNs + OAuth surface area. Two day jobs means the company is nobody's main thing during the exact window they've declared decisive. And the "fully built product before any demand signal" is itself the anti-pattern: two months of supply-side work, zero months of demand-side work, now retro-fitting a market test.

### Verdict

**Three most fatal flaws, ranked:**
1. **The two-sided adoption paradox.** The differentiating value requires the ex-spouse — a definitionally uncooperative counterparty — to install, pay-share, and grant data access; incumbents solve this via court mandate, a channel this team cannot reach. Without parent #2, it collapses into a generic family organizer competing with free.
2. **Broken unit economics on the only available channel, compounded by structural churn.** CAC ~$220 vs. net LTV ~$210–240 (LTV:CAC ≈ 1), driven by a seasonal panic-purchase whose value is extracted in month one of a 12-month prepay — and the 90-day demand test (July–October) sits entirely outside the camp-panic season it depends on.
3. **No moat against platform absorption or incumbents.** The flyer/email-to-calendar core is being shipped free by Google and Apple; the whole product was rebuilt-able in two founder-months, and OFW/Skylight can add it as a feature to installed bases this team can't buy access to. Trust/regulatory drag (CASA gating the Gmail feature, custody-data subpoena exposure) makes the unknown-brand version strictly worse than the incumbent version.

**Viability score: 3/10.** The pain is real, the buyer is trained to pay, and the founder-customer fit is genuine — that's what keeps it off the floor. But the product's differentiation requires an adversary's consent, its economics are at parity before salaries, its wedge is out of season for the decision window, and its core trick is being given away by the platforms it's built on. As specified — this team, this budget, this timeline, this GTM — it does not survive contact with the market.

## B. Expansionist (8/10)

### 1. Why the ceiling is higher than it looks

The pitch says "camp logistics for divorced parents." What's actually been built is something much rarer: **a persistent, structured, AI-readable model of a family** — kids, ages, interests, custody rhythm, schedules, providers, conflicts, coverage gaps — plus the pipes (Gmail read, GCal two-way sync, photo/email ingestion) that keep that model alive without manual data entry. Every consumer AI company wants "memory about your life." Almost none have a wedge painful enough to make users *grant the permissions and keep the data fresh*. Camp panic + custody chaos is that wedge. The family graph is the company.

Second structural gift: **the two-household market is the hardest version of the problem, which means everything downstream is easier.** If the product can coordinate across two adults who may not even speak to each other, coordinating one household of two cooperating adults is a strict subset. You're building on hard mode; expansion is de-escalation.

Third: the incumbents prove willingness to pay at a shocking level. OurFamilyWizard charges ~$150–250 *per parent per year*, is often *court-ordered*, and is — by near-universal user testimony — a resentment-logging tool, not a helper. It documents conflict; this product *removes the reasons for conflict* (who signed up for what, who's covering Thursday). $199/yr covering both parents undercuts the incumbent pair-price by 50–70% while delivering categorically more value. That's not a feature war; that's a generational replacement.

### 2. Adjacent markets, in order of adjacency

**Ring 1 — All coordinating families (10–20x TAM).** ~1M US divorces/yr involving kids and several million active co-parenting households, vs ~30M+ US households with kids under 13. The "maxed-out coordinating mom" secondary buyer is actually the mainstream market wearing a disguise. The single-household version is the same product minus the second-household handshake. Cozi (tens of millions of registered users, ad-supported, stagnant, zero AI) shows the audience exists and is unserved by anything intelligent. Skylight ($300M+ revenue on *calendar hardware* for families) proves families pay real money for coordination — with a dumb screen. This company is the software brain Skylight never built.

**Ring 2 — School-life and youth-sports admin.** The flyer-photo → tracked-activity pipeline generalizes instantly: school emails, permission slips, spirit days, snack schedules, tournament brackets. Youth sports alone is a ~$30–40B US spend with notoriously chaotic logistics (TeamSnap/GameChanger own the *team* side; nobody owns the *family* side aggregating three kids across five orgs). Parents don't want six apps per kid; they want one inbox-to-calendar brain. This is a feature-flag expansion, not a new product.

**Ring 3 — Sandwich generation / eldercare.** Same primitives — multi-party coordination across households, appointment ingestion, coverage-gap detection, a persistent profile of a person you're responsible for — pointed at aging parents instead of kids. The buyer is literally the same 40-something woman. Eldercare coordination is a massive, guilt-soaked, underserved spend, and "the chief-of-staff for everyone you're responsible for" is the natural brand arc.

**Ring 4 — B2B2C distribution channels, not new products:**
- **Family courts and mediators**: OFW's genius was court-ordered distribution. A cooperative-coordination app that demonstrably reduces scheduling disputes is something judges and mediators can recommend *instead of* or alongside conflict-documentation apps. One line in standard parenting-plan templates = zero-CAC recurring distribution. Family-law attorneys (~50K+ US practices) become a referral army.
- **Employers/EAPs**: "working-parent burnout" is a named benefits category (Care.com's enterprise arm, Cleo, Maven). A $199 benefit that visibly reduces parental cognitive load is an easy EAP add-on; one mid-size employer deal = thousands of seats.
- **Pediatricians/therapists**: divorce-adjustment counselors and family therapists are trusted-recommender nodes for exactly this buyer at exactly the moment of purchase intent.

### 3. Platform potential

**The camp/activity marketplace is the real monetization iceberg.** The AI research agent already discovers and scores local camps/activities. The US camp market alone is ~$3–4B in registrations, youth activities broadly far more, and the supply side is thousands of small providers with terrible marketing who pay real money (Sawyer, ActivityHero, CampMinder exist but none own *demand*). Today the product finds camps; tomorrow it books them (take rate: 5–10% of a $400 camp registration is $20–40 per booking — a single summer of 3 camps × 2 kids can exceed the annual subscription); the day after, providers pay for placement, fill-the-last-8-spots yield tools, and demand analytics ("47 families within 5 miles want a Tuesday 4pm art class for 6-year-olds — no one offers it"). The company would hold the only *intent-and-fit-scored* demand graph for local kids' activities. That's the OpenTable/Faire move: start as the tool, become the market.

**The family profile as an API.** Longer-term and speculative but enormous: a consented, parent-controlled "family context" layer that other services (tutoring, babysitting, insurance, travel, pediatric telehealth) can query with permission. As agentic AI becomes how households transact, whoever holds the trusted family profile is the router. Plaid-for-families is a real endgame.

**Provider-side SaaS** falls out for free: the same lifecycle/CRM tooling built for the consumer side, sold to camps to manage rosters, comms, and re-enrollment — anchored by the demand you send them.

### 4. Compounding assets

1. **The two-household coordination graph** — no one else has structured data on how custody schedules, activities, and handoffs actually interact. It trains better conflict-detection, better recommendations, and is genuinely hard to replicate (requires both parents' consent — a two-sided cold-start moat that, once crossed, is a two-sided *lock-in*: churning requires two people to agree).
2. **Proprietary local-activity data** — AI-researched, parent-verified, fit-scored inventory of camps/classes that Google doesn't structure and Yelp doesn't cover. Refreshed by usage.
3. **Trust brand in family AI** — the first company parents trust with kid data + AI is positioned for everything else in the category. Trust is the scarcest asset in family tech and it compounds with every safe year.
4. **Founder-customer authenticity → audience.** A divorced engineer dad + maxed-out mom co-founder building in public for co-parents is a content machine waiting to be turned on. Zero audience today is a gap, not a ceiling — the story is pre-written.
5. **AI-native cost structure**: solo-engineer ship speed + low infra means expansion experiments (eldercare mode, sports mode, marketplace pilot) cost weeks, not funding rounds. The option portfolio is nearly free to hold.

### 5. The 100x version: what is this at $100M+ ARR?

**"The operating system for the modern family"** — the trusted AI layer that runs household logistics and transacts on the family's behalf. Revenue stack at $100M+: ~1.5–2M subscribing households at a blended ~$180/yr (consumer + employer-paid seats) → ~$300M subscription; marketplace take on activity/camp bookings: 500K booking households × $1,000/yr activity spend × 7% → ~$35M; provider tools + placement → $15–25M; court/EAP channel margin on top.

**Explicit assumptions that must be TRUE:**
1. The co-parent wedge converts: two-household families adopt at ≥3–5% of the addressable segment and *both* parents activate (the single hardest assumption — one-price-both-parents is the right knife, but the second parent's consent is the funnel's choke point).
2. Retention is genuinely carried by the coach + always-fresh calendar — i.e., the product survives the end of camp season (must see 6-month retention >70% in beta cohorts).
3. Gmail/Calendar-level ingestion stays available (Google API/CASA compliance navigated — already in flight) and Apple/Google don't sherlock the category faster than the family-graph moat compounds. The defense is the *two-household graph and the marketplace*, which platform vendors structurally won't build.
4. Expansion from two-household → all-household actually happens without brand pigeonholing ("the divorce app" stigma is manageable via a master brand + wedge sub-positioning).
5. The marketplace supply side is buildable city-by-city — the classic local-marketplace grind, but the AI research agent is a supply-bootstrapping cheat code no prior entrant had.

### 6. The dormant asset: per-kid AI voice companions

A hidden second company. Already built: voice companions with safety/moderation systems, crisis detection with parent push alerts — i.e., the *safety infrastructure* everyone else in kids' AI is missing. The kids' AI companion space is currently a reputational minefield (Character.AI lawsuits, COPPA scrutiny) — which is precisely why a parent-controlled, family-profile-grounded, moderation-first companion from a *trusted parenting brand* could own it when the regulatory dust settles. Concrete forms: a comfort/continuity companion for kids moving between two houses (deeply on-thesis — "you're at Dad's Thursday, want to make a list of what to pack?"); reading/homework help; and — most defensibly — **licensing the kid-safety/moderation stack itself** to other kids' AI products once regulation makes such infrastructure mandatory. Optionality value: potentially larger than the core product, correctly held dark until trust and regulation mature.

### Conclusion

**(1) Single most valuable expansion path:** Land the divorced-co-parent wedge (with the family-court/mediator channel as zero-CAC distribution), then expand concentrically into **all coordinating families and the camp/activity booking marketplace** — subscription funds the land-grab, marketplace take-rate and the proprietary family/activity graph are where the $100M+ ARR and the durable moat live.

**(2) Upside score: 8/10.** Genuine platform-scale outcome available, docked from 9–10 because the ceiling depends on two hard crossings: the second-parent activation choke point, and the two-household → mainstream brand jump.

## C. First-Principles (5/10)

### 1. What job is being done, and for whom exactly?

The product bundles five distinct jobs: **(a) Discovery** (episodic research, 2–4 bursts/yr, bounded pain); **(b) Extraction/capture** (friction removal, ~3–10 min saved per instance, seasonal frequency); **(c) Coordination** ("make sure two households collectively cover every kid, every day, no conflicts, no gaps") — the *only* job where failure is catastrophic and emotionally loaded, and for divorced co-parents failures carry a second-order cost: **ammunition in an ongoing adversarial relationship**; **(d) Deciding** (a two-party negotiation software can inform but not do); **(e) Advice** (the coach — a *wants* job, not a *needs* job).

**Who exactly:** the *organized parent* in a two-household arrangement — the one who carries the mental load. The customer is **one person**, not the dyad. **The hair-on-fire job is (c) coordination-with-gap-detection — the fear of the dropped ball across a boundary of low trust.** Discovery is the stated wedge, but it's a nice-to-have wearing the hair-on-fire job's clothes: the panic in "camp signup panic" is "I must solve a 10-week coverage matrix across two households before slots fill, and if I get it wrong it's my fault."

### 2. What do these people do today, and what does it cost?

Today's stack: memory + camera roll, a semi-shared Google Calendar, terse text threads with the ex, a spreadsheet built each March and abandoned by June, the school email deluge, and (higher-conflict cases) a court-ordered documentation app. Cost: ~80–120 hrs/yr for the load-bearing parent, $50–300/yr in missed deadlines/deposits, and — the dominant, unpriceable cost — conflict: every schedule ambiguity is a potential fight. But note the perverse feature: **the mess is survivable**; hair is on fire *seasonally* and always burns out on its own. That's why the spreadsheet gets abandoned by June.

### 3. Physics of the value exchange

Plausible family (2 kids, 60/40 custody, both parents working): time saved ≈ 25–30 hrs/yr → $750–900 at a $30/hr shadow wage, plus one prevented dropped-ball ($100–300 + a fight avoided), plus a high emotional-stakes multiplier. **$199/yr clears the rational bar 4–5x for the load-bearing parent alone.** The price is not the problem.

But the two-party physics: cross-household gap detection only works with both calendars in the system. Value with one parent connected: discovery ✓, extraction ✓, own-calendar ✓, coach ✓, **cross-household gap detection ✗**. The two-party requirement transforms the sale into a *negotiation* with the person the buyer least wants to owe a favor — an ask that in a low-trust relationship reads as surveillance ("she wants a record of my scheduling failures"). The asymmetry is fatal: the disorganized parent, whose gaps the system would surface, has the *least* incentive to make their gaps visible. **The system's chief value to the buyer is precisely the thing the second user has reason to resist.** "One price covers both" solves the wrong problem — money was never the second parent's objection. Honest framing: **a single-player tool with a two-player aspiration**, currently marketed on the two-player promise.

### 4. The causal chain, link by link

1. Product exists — ✓. 2. Target parent hears about it — fragile but workable (seasonal discovery window). 3. **Believes it enough to connect Gmail + calendar — very weak**: "grant an unknown two-person company read access to your entire email," asked of a person whose life contains an adversary who would love their private data, possibly amid litigation; the perceived downside of a leak is *discovery in family court*. The product's magic sits behind its scariest permission (mitigable if onboarding leads with flyer-photo capture instead). 4. First-use value — strong *in season*, hollow out of season. 5. Habit — weak: the need pulses; between pulses the product's outputs live in Google Calendar, so **success = invisibility** — a tool that writes everything into Google Calendar teaches the user they only need Google Calendar. 6. Pays after trial — moderate, in-season only; expect "I'll subscribe next March." 7. Stays 12+ months — weak: **the retention job is assigned to the bundle's weakest-demand component (the coach)** — if the coach were strong enough to retain, it would be strong enough to be the wedge; it isn't, which is why it isn't. 8. Tells other parents — potentially strong (every activated family has a built-in second household to convert), but inherits 5/7's weakness.

**Weakest link: #3 (the trust/permission gate)** — earliest in the chain, so every downstream pass-rate multiplies by it, and intrinsic: the core mechanism (read my email, see both households) is maximally invasive for the population (post-divorce, litigation-adjacent) least able to extend trust.

### 5. Internal contradictions (descending severity)

1. **The moat repels its own required user** — two-household coordination is the differentiation, but the second household contains the person least likely to adopt, and the value delivered (making gaps visible and attributable) is a *cost* to the parent who creates the gaps.
2. **The retention job is assigned to the weakest product** (the coach, commoditized by free general assistants).
3. **Drowning buyer, swim-lesson onboarding** — the pitch is "you have no bandwidth"; the onboarding is connect Gmail + Calendar + build a profile + recruit your ex.
4. **Success = invisibility** — two-way sync moves the product's value into Google's UI; annual renewal arrives after 8 months of not opening the app.

In the idea's favor: founder-customer fit is real, single-player economics clear the bar without the ex, and camp panic is a correct, timeable acquisition wedge. The idea is coherent about the *pain* and inverted about the *mechanism*: it sells a two-player, always-on, high-trust product to a one-player, seasonal, low-trust buyer.

**(1) Weakest link:** the trust/permission gate, compounded by the second-parent adoption requirement. **(2) Coherence score: 5/10.**

## E. Buyer (5/10, "maybe later")

*(First-person roleplay: 41-year-old divorced dad, two kids 10 and 5, 50/50 custody, ~$120K HHI, already paying $216/yr for court-required OurFamilyWizard.)*

**1. Gut reaction and first objection.** Whoever wrote this pitch has lived my life — "a week where neither of you has coverage" cost me a vacation day last July. The flyer-photo thing hits: the flyer goes home in my daughter's backpack on HER week, dies in my ex's kitchen, and I learn about the camp when the Venmo request lands. But my FIRST objection, before price: **this only works if my ex participates, and I cannot make my ex participate.** Every co-parenting tool dies on this rock. OFW only works because a judge made it work. If I sign up and she doesn't — and the school has HER as primary contact, so she has all the raw material — I've bought a $199/yr dashboard of my own ignorance. Second objection: "reads camp emails with permission" mostly needs permission to *her* inbox. Good luck.

**2. Comparisons.** OFW ($432/yr combined) isn't really the same product — it's a court-admissible log that has never found me a camp; and my custody agreement means MVP is $199 *on top of* $432, not instead — the OFW price comparison is a little dishonest for anyone court-ordered. Google Calendar ($0) fails because nobody enters anything — fair point to them. ChatGPT free tier: I've literally pasted the rec-department camp PDF into it; worked okay, but I still had to calendar everything manually — so the AI alone isn't worth $199; the *plumbing* is the product. Cozi/Skylight anchor my price expectation for "family calendar app" way below $199.

**3. The awkward questions.** (a) *Does my ex have to join?* Tell me what solo mode actually does, because solo mode is what I'm realistically buying for six months. If I can forward emails and snap flyers with her as a read-only invite recipient who never signed up, there's a wedge. If gap detection requires her to maintain her calendar in your app — it'll never be maintained. (b) *Data:* you hold my custody schedule, kids' profiles, and an AI coach's transcripts of me venting about co-parenting. Three fears: the startup dies (one guy's side project sunsets with a two-week email); **subpoena — if we go back to court, can her lawyer subpoena my coach chats? Probably yes, so I will never type an honest word into it**; training data. I need: export in a standard format, deletion guarantees, retention policy, a 90-day sunset promise. (c) *Why not ChatGPT + calendar?* The ingestion + two-household sync is a real answer. The coach IS just ChatGPT, I'm not paying for it, and it creates the subpoena problem — **I'd pay more for the product with the coach removed.**

**4. Price.** $199/family is not insulting — it's one camp-registration fee, and "the coverage-gap flag saves you one vacation day = $500" lands. Fair: **$99–149/yr**, or $199 with a real solo mode. **5. What gets my card out TODAY.** The demo, in the sales flow, before paying: I forward one real camp email or snap one flyer, connect my calendar, enter our custody rotation (it better handle 2-2-3, holidays, and swap weeks), and within two minutes I see the camp tracked, on my calendar, with a deadline to-do, AND a red bar across the summer: "June 15–19: neither home has coverage, here are 4 camps with openings for a 10-year-old." **Show me MY kids' actual gap on MY actual calendar in the free trial and I'm converted on the spot.** Plus: full refund before September 1 ("if this doesn't survive one real summer, you pay nothing"); the data promise in writing; and show me the exact invite my ex receives — if it's a Google Calendar invite plus "reply to add stuff," I believe it; if it's "download the app and complete onboarding," I know how that ends.
