### What the books contribute—and what we have actually read

The supplied photograph identifies Andrew D. Huberman's *Protocols: An Operating Manual for the Human Body* and Christopher M. Palmer's *Brain Energy*. I reviewed official descriptions, not the full books. Huberman's description emphasizes practical routines across physical and mental performance. Palmer proposes a metabolic framework for mental illness. They supply ideas to investigate, not validation for every recommendation a future coach might make. There is clinical research relevant to the interest in nutrition. The SMILES randomized trial investigated dietary improvement as an adjunct for adults with depression and reported promising results while calling for larger replication. That supports investigating nutrition's role; it does not establish a universal explanation of mental illness or the efficacy of an AI-delivered program. [Original SMILES trial](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-017-0791-y).

A July 2026 ketogenic-diet study randomized 58 people with schizophrenia-spectrum or bipolar-I disorders for one month; a 25-person subgroup continued for four months. Its abstract reports between-group metabolic improvements at one month and symptom improvements against baseline in the extension. Those comparisons are different: the longer-term symptom result should not be presented as a four-month randomized treatment effect. This is a useful illustration of why reading the design matters more than repeating the headline. This assessment reviewed the abstract, not a full systematic evidence base. [Study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42415343/).

Existing depression guidance also considers sleep, diet, activity, and social context, so “whole person” should not be marketed as something all conventional care ignores. The opportunity to test is how well the product integrates useful guidance into everyday life. [NICE recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG222/chapter/recommendations).

### The potential product advantage

The hypothesis is that people struggle to reconcile advice and sustain useful changes across domains. A coach could help them decide what matters now, choose a manageable action, fit it into their actual circumstances, and revisit the result. More information is not always helpful; sometimes the valuable response is to simplify.

An illustrative interaction: an adult says they keep losing patience at home and feel depleted at work. The coach asks about their goals, daily demands, routines, and existing support rather than immediately declaring a cause. Together they choose one feasible change and one communication practice. With permission, the coach helps schedule them and checks back. Persistent or concerning symptoms lead to professional evaluation, not a confident claim that metabolism or mindset explains everything.

Possible first research audience: adults who already consume health/growth material, feel overwhelmed by conflicting advice, and want help implementing a small number of everyday practices. A proposed four-week outcome is improved self-reported energy or day-to-day functioning alongside actual practice completion. This is an unvalidated offer; such changes would not, by themselves, establish clinical efficacy or causality.

### “Research-informed” needs an editorial process

The science function should discover new work, appraise it, and update a reviewed practice library. The conversational coach should use that library and explain uncertainty. Browsing the newest paper during a vulnerable conversation is insufficient governance.

Each practice needs a defined purpose, population, evidence strength, relevant limitations, exclusions, burden, and review date. Distinguish guideline-supported practices, replicated human evidence, early trials, mechanisms/animal research, and personal or spiritual philosophy. Scientific plausibility does not establish clinical benefit. Newer research is not automatically better research.

Clinical claims or recommendations need qualified review, conflict-of-interest checks, and a versioned record of what changed and why. A new study might justify updating an explanation or preparing a question for a clinician without justifying an intervention. Cost this continuing work explicitly; it is part of the product, not a one-time prompt-writing task.

Medication changes, therapeutic diets for psychiatric illness, withdrawal management, and treatment of serious symptoms belong in a separately designed clinical service with appropriate professionals. An ordinary coaching product can help someone understand information, prepare questions, and follow an existing clinician-agreed plan. Calling everything coaching does not resolve the difference.

### Spirituality and science can coexist with clear attribution

The coach can support a person's chosen meaning, values, gratitude, contemplative practice, or spiritual tradition without claiming that neuroscience proves a metaphysical belief. Ask what framing the person wants. Distinguish evidence about a practice from the interpretation someone gives it. Do not imply that illness reflects insufficient gratitude, spiritual development, or effort.

### How this changes the live-agent idea

Use live multimodality where it helps a person practice or implement something: rehearse a boundary, work through a planning document, or review a routine together. Persistent context and sensible follow-up may matter more than camera use in every session. Test a live-capable longitudinal coach against an otherwise comparable voice/text version.

“Superhuman coach” is a useful expression of ambition, but an overbroad launch promise. The testable claim is narrower: a person receives coherent, evidence-informed help that fits their circumstances and improves a chosen aspect of daily life. “Get better faster” requires a defined outcome, comparison, and time window; it is not yet an established benefit of this concept.

## The coach with interactive practice tools

Miguel's next refinement makes the proposed product more tangible: the agent can bring up small interactive tools that help people understand, perform, and track an agreed practice. This updates the earlier “no UI” framing. The intended experience is conversational, with useful controls appearing when needed, rather than a large dashboard the user must learn.

The proposed loop is **conversation → choose a practice → use a tool → record what happened → review together**. The coach and tools share one user-approved plan. A completed timer does not automatically mean an exercise was performed or helped; distinguish tool events, user reports, and measured outcomes.

Illustrative tools to evaluate, not an approved feature list:

- **Routine builder:** fit a chosen practice into the person's week, with an adjustable minimum version for difficult days.
- **Guided practice:** a short original reflection or rehearsal, with prompts, optional audio, and a timer where useful.
- **Plan-to-action card:** one next step, chosen time, reminder preference, and easy rescheduling.
- **Practice log:** done, partly done, skipped, or not recorded; optional context about effort and obstacles.
- **Progress review:** show consistency alongside the person's chosen outcome, perceived benefit, burden, and any unwanted effects.
- **Evidence card:** explain what the practice is for, the strength and limits of its support, and when professional input is needed.

A sample interaction: “I read about routines, but I never stick with them.” The coach asks what the person wants to improve and what their evenings actually look like. They select a small routine; the tool helps place it in the week. After several attempts, the review shows both participation and the person's experience. The agent might recommend simplifying, continuing, stopping, or asking a professional, rather than congratulating a streak regardless of results.

This strengthens the hypothesis of implementation support. It does not establish that people want another tracker. Test whether in-conversation tools reduce work and improve useful follow-through compared with the same coach plus ordinary reminders or notes. Keep measures small enough that logging does not become the job.

### Design commitments worth testing

1. The person chooses the goal and approves the practice. The agent does not continually prescribe new optimization tasks.
2. Consistency, benefit, and burden are separate. Missing a day is information, not a moral failure. Missing data is not a failed practice.
3. Personal trends are descriptive. A before/after chart cannot establish the cause of a health change, especially when several things changed together.
4. Tools expose their state and support correction. The agent cannot claim a practice was completed merely because it generated instructions.
5. An evidence update does not silently change an active routine. Show the change and obtain agreement; clinical plans require the appropriate professional involvement.
6. Implement original or properly licensed exercises. Inspiration from *Protocols* or *Brain Energy* is not authorization to copy their text, artwork, or branded programs, nor evidence of endorsement.

A small set of tested tools is preferable for an initial product. An agent could select and configure them conversationally; generating arbitrary executable health tools during each session would introduce a different verification problem. The number of tools and their implementation remain undecided.

### Revised first prototype hypothesis

One adult-facing coach, one selected everyday-wellbeing outcome, two or three reviewed practices, and a few interactive components that make those practices easier to use. Measure completion, perceived usefulness, burden, return, and payment. Live voice/video remains a delivery option to compare rather than a requirement for every tool.

This can preserve the broad whole-person mission while giving the first trial a coherent job: help someone put a small, evidence-informed plan into their actual life and decide whether it is worth continuing. It remains a hypothesis requiring customer and outcome evidence.

### An earlier candidate, now one capability to compare

**A live coach for rehearsing one difficult conversation before it happens.** This was my initial live-agent recommendation. The later whole-person clarification broadens the investigation; do not treat rehearsal as a settled product direction.

Example first audience: first-time engineering managers preparing to give specific, respectful feedback. This is a recruitment hypothesis grounded in your experience, not an established market conclusion. A parenting alternative is an adult preparing to discuss a recurring school issue with a teacher. Do not launch both at once.

One useful session could work like this:

1. The adult describes the situation and desired outcome, and optionally shares a redacted draft or relevant document.
2. The coach checks facts and asks what the other person's perspective might be. It does not pretend to know that person's thoughts.
3. The agent plays the counterpart in a clearly labeled simulation. The adult practices speaking and responding to pushback.
4. The coach pauses, cites a specific moment, offers one adjustment, and lets the adult retry. It distinguishes an observation from an interpretation.
5. They leave with a short opening, a question to ask, a boundary or request, and a next step. A user-approved follow-up checks what actually happened.

This connects your interest in emotional awareness with a concrete act of growth. It also has a useful stopping point: the person leaves ready to have the real conversation. Success is not time spent with an avatar.

But this is not empty competitive space. Yoodli advertises customizable roleplays, feedback, screen sharing, and optional camera-based feedback. That makes it a necessary baseline, not a footnote. A candidate distinction would be an original approach for one audience with follow-up into real-world practice; its value remains to be proven. [Yoodli roleplays](https://support.yoodli.ai/en/articles/11565137-how-to-build-and-customize-roleplays), [practice capabilities](https://support.yoodli.ai/en/articles/9550465-practice-with-yoodli).

### Make the camera earn its place

Voice is intrinsic to practicing a spoken conversation. A draft, diagram, or screen share can supply useful context. A camera facing the user may help with selected observable presentation behaviors, but does not reliably reveal feelings, honesty, diagnosis, or intention. Do not sell emotional mind-reading. A generated avatar may make practice feel natural—or distracting. Test it separately.

If live visual input must be central rather than optional, investigate an adult-guided family routine setup session: show a workspace or morning preparation area, choose one recurring bottleneck, rearrange it with guidance, and produce a usable routine. The camera can reveal changing objects and layout without filming a child. However, willingness to pay and repeat use are weaker unknowns. Keep this as a competing hypothesis, not a second launch feature.

Generic see-and-talk capability is already available: Google's consumer documentation describes Gemini Live camera/screen help, and its developer documentation supports real-time audio/video interaction. We would be selling a better specific outcome, not access to multimodality itself. [Gemini Live camera](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-live-camera-how-to/), [Live API](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api/get-started-sdk).

### What “very strong” should mean in a test

- It handles interruptions and uncertain input without losing the thread.
- It accepts corrections and uses them consistently in the resulting plan.
- Feedback refers to something the user actually said or showed; it does not invent evidence.
- The person improves against a task-specific rubric and can use the result outside the app.
- Audio-only, live video, and avatar variants are compared independently; more technology does not automatically score higher.
- Reconnection, noisy audio, delays, and tool failures do not silently erase progress or produce false completion.
- Cost is measured per successful session, including follow-up and human support.

The next research should establish which moment deserves that quality. We have not selected a model, avatar vendor, platform, or build architecture.

## The biggest lessons from MVP

### 1. We learned how to build; we did not establish what people buy

MVP shipped a substantial system to a handful of beta families. The paywall did not charge. The supplied record does not establish paid retention or repeatable acquisition. This is neither proof the market rejected parenting nor proof that one more simplification would produce demand.

The next experiment must distinguish a pleasant experience from payment, repeated use, and a result the user values.

### 2. A large research effort can still miss the decisive evidence

The August work had no parent interviews or fielded survey. Its red team showed how counting repeated attendances as capture events inflated the central ratio. The corrected comparison was roughly 23 low-step obligations versus 16 multi-step campaigns, not 18:1. It also found no willingness-to-pay evidence for capture.

The enduring lesson is to define the unit of value correctly. Message counts, check-ins, app opens, and number of agents are not outcomes. For a growth product, measure attempted and completed practices. For a parent operator, measure the job completed correctly and the effort still required from the parent.

Sources: [project context](../parent-app-context.md), [August red team](research/2026-08-parent-planning-behavior/raw/S4-redteam.md). The red team includes earlier superseded figures to explain its critique; use the context and corrected research brief for final historical figures.

### 3. Capture is an entrance, not automatically a business

A voice note, screenshot, or forwarded email is valuable when it starts a useful sequence. The stronger sequence is capture → clarify → agree → act → verify → revisit. Generic capture competes with tools people already use.

### 4. More surface area creates more ways to lose trust

MVP accumulated coaching, planning, family communication, content, a kid companion, multiple onboarding paths, and voice/text threads with separate memory. Some state changes failed silently or left views stale. For an agent with less visible structure, durable state and visible action receipts become even more important.

This is a reason to narrow the first promise, not merely remove tabs.

### 5. A second person's participation is a cost

A parent should receive useful help alone. Likewise, a growth customer should not need a therapist, partner, coach, wearable, and inbox connection before getting value. Optional collaboration should follow individual value.

### 6. Voice findings are contextual

The August corpus favored fast capture over conversational voice for family planning. That does not prove voice is unhelpful for private reflection. Test voice notes, live voice, and text for the specific job. Measure useful result per session and cost, rather than assuming one modality wins everywhere.

### 7. Reuse should follow the job

Potential assets include durable agent state, repair/retry lessons, planning tools, calendar handling, flyer extraction, and evaluation infrastructure. Potential liabilities include family-specific storage assumptions, multiple conversation systems, legacy auth, and domain-specific safety logic. These are inventory hypotheses, not an audited reuse plan.

Now that the team arrangement is changing, establish rights to shared code, branding, designs, and editorial content before relying on them in a new product. No determination of ownership is made here.

## Where I would draw the initial scope

I would not launch with an “addiction sponsor” persona. That name invites a particularly consequential expectation of human-like availability and judgment. A future recovery-support tool should be designed with recovery practitioners and people with lived experience, clearly supplement human support, and have a separately evaluated scope. The same restraint applies to individualized medical nutrition or exercise advice in the presence of illness or injury.

This does not mean useful AI coaching is impossible. Dartmouth's Therabot trial provides promising evidence for a specific system under a specific study design. The researchers emphasize clinician oversight; it does not validate a generic multi-agent coach or establish a consumer business. [Dartmouth trial summary](https://home.dartmouth.edu/news/2025/03/first-therapy-chatbot-trial-yields-mental-health-benefits).

A first pilot could focus on adult goal clarification, preparing a conversation, reflecting on a setback, or following through on a self-chosen everyday practice. Keep recovery treatment, diagnosis, and clinical claims outside that pilot. FDA's general-wellness guidance distinguishes low-risk healthy-lifestyle functions from disease-related functions; the exact intended use and claims need review before launch. The FTC's companion inquiry also makes persona design and engagement incentives relevant. These sources are boundaries for investigation, not a legal classification of the proposed product. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices), [FTC inquiry](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions).

## A small experiment that can change our minds

Use two recruitment offers, not two built applications. With the live-agent refinement, prioritize a concrete rehearsal scenario in each; compare live coaching with asynchronous support and existing tools before committing to video:

1. **Parenting:** “Bring one difficult recurring family situation. Leave with a plan you can use, and support following through.” Pick one situation class after interviews.
2. **Growth:** “Bring one change you keep thinking about. Turn it into a workable practice and review what happened over four weeks.”

Recruit about 8–10 adults per offer with a real recent example, not only friends who want to support you. Start with problem interviews, then invite qualified people into a transparent paid concierge pilot. A proposed $49 four-week fee is a test assumption, not a researched optimum. Disclose exactly what is AI-assisted and what you personally review. No fake automation, no health-treatment claims, no need to grant inbox access at signup.

For each participant, establish a baseline, choose one action, obtain permission for follow-up, and check what actually happened. Compare with their current workflow and a competently configured general assistant. Record your time as well as compute cost.

Before recruitment, pre-register provisional gates. For example: of 10 qualified people offered a pilot, at least five pay; at least three of those five complete a meaningful week-four action cycle and pay to continue; most continuing participants can name a benefit they did not get from their prior approach. These small counts are directional decision rules, not statistical validation. Track everyone offered the pilot, not just enthusiastic finishers. A serious harmful failure pauses the affected scope regardless of conversion.

Favor the direction that combines payment, repeat outcomes, reachable customers, manageable support, and your desire to do the work. If neither passes, change the job or stop. Do not answer weak demand by adding specialists.
