### Interpretation

**1. The acute pain is the RACE, not the CHOICE — and the sourced evidence supports this asymmetrically.**
Every well-methoded source measures *access* failure, not *selection* failure: 24% of enrolled children were waitlisted first (#6); 77% unmet demand (#7); 48% of parents wanted participation they could not get (#4); 60% of caregivers had access difficulty cost them work (#10). By contrast, nothing in the Tier A/B corpus shows parents struggling to *decide which camp*. LendingTree (#17) even shows selection is settled cheaply — child's interest (30%) and cost (25%). This is the strongest strategic signal in my domain: **a discovery/fit-scoring agent is solving the cheap half of the problem.** The expensive half is knowing the exact date and minute a specific provider opens, having the form pre-filled, and not missing it. The product already has the right primitive for this — the registration-window watchlist with push notifications — and it is buried behind a 12-knob discovery tab.

**2. Summer is a Q1 purchase with a hard, provider-set clock.** 32% of parents start 3+ months out (#21); registrations cluster in late Jan–mid Feb with staggered priority windows (#27); popular sessions clear in 90 seconds (#26). The task has a *seasonal spike*, not a steady load. That has a direct product consequence: a camp-first app is a January–April app for most households. Episode frequency is low; episode stakes are high. That argues against subscription framing anchored on camp alone and for camp being the *acquisition* wedge inside a year-round product.

**3. The task has a hard age ceiling that maps onto the cohorts.** Overnight camp is only 11% of K-12 children (#2); by high school the modal summer activity is a job (#3). Gen X parents of teens are, in large part, **not in this market at all** — their summer job is transport, jobs, and college-adjacent logistics. Millennial parents of elementary/middle kids are the entire load-bearing segment (#3, #13). Gen Z parents are structurally absent: their children are mostly pre-K, where the problem is daycare continuity, and day care is only 6% of K-12 summer arrangements (#2). **No US source I could find breaks camp behavior out by parent generation.** If the product's camp vertical is the default-on door, it is default-on for exactly one of the three cohorts.

**4. The coverage gap is arithmetic, and the app can compute it.** Schools close 10–12 weeks; programming covers ~5 (#22); 66.3% of married-couple families have two working parents (#24); 60% use up to 2 weeks of PTO on closures (#25). The unit of the job is therefore **"which weeks am I uncovered,"** not "which camp is good." The shipped Planner's conflict + coverage-gap detection is the single feature most aligned with the sourced pain; the discovery agent is the least aligned. Beyond summer, the school calendar generates a second, unaddressed stream: ~185 non-school days a year against a 180-day school year (#23), most of them one- and two-day closures that no camp covers.

**5. Explicit contradictions between sources.**
(a) *Cost per child.* LendingTree says $893/child for summer (2025, n=638); CAP says $3,000+ for two children ≈ $1,500/child (2018, from 2013 AA3PM data). These are ~1.7x apart in the wrong direction over time — CAP's is a *modeled full-coverage* cost while LendingTree's is *observed actual spend*, which is lower precisely because families under-purchase. Both are real; they measure different things. Do not average them.
(b) *Participation.* Gallup says 55% of K-12 children were in ≥1 structured summer program (#1); LendingTree says only 33% used day camps and 60% "require" summer care (#12, #16). Gallup's definition is broader (enrichment, summer school, jobs all count).
(c) *Waitlist prevalence.* The 24% waitlist figure (#6) is an **afterschool** measure, not a camp measure. I am using it as the best-methoded proxy for camp scarcity because no equivalent camp-side survey is publicly retrievable. Flag this in synthesis — it is a borrowed number.

**6. Two things I could NOT source, which are findings.**
(a) **No US survey quantifies the hours a parent spends researching and booking summer care.** I searched for it directly and traced every promising lead. Proxies exist for *elapsed* time (52% search 2+ months; 32% start 3 months out) but not for *hours consumed*. Any cross-task scorecard that puts an hours figure against camps is fabricating it.
(b) **No sourced data on what tool parents use.** The "summer camp spreadsheet" is a real cultural artifact but I found zero survey measurement of its prevalence. The only measured tool fact is Care.com's "~3 apps/websites to find care" (#18), which is about discovery, not tracking.

**7. American Camp Association primary data was not retrievable.** acacamps.org (Camp Trends: Enrollment, ACA Facts & Trends, and the Sawyer-sponsored 2026 Camp Booking & Pricing Trends post) is behind Cloudflare bot protection and returned 403 to every access path I attempted. The widely-circulated ACA figures — "26 million campers," "14,000+ day and resident camps (8,400 resident / 5,600 day)" — appear only in secondary quotation with no retrievable methodology or fielding year, so they go to WEAK SIGNAL. ACA's own CampCounts 2025 dashboard is camp-member-gated and was scheduled for Spring 2026 release. **The best US camp-participation numbers available to this study are Gallup's (#1–#5), not ACA's** — and Gallup is the better source anyway (probability panel, n=6,899, published MoE).

## Section 2: WEAK SIGNAL

- **"26 million children and adults attend camp each summer" / "more than 14,000 day and resident camps (8,400 resident, 5,600 day)"** — American Camp Association. Circulates everywhere; I could not retrieve ACA's own page (403 Cloudflare) and no secondary citation carries a fielding year or method. The 26M figure appears to predate 2020. **Unverifiable as cited; do not use.** Use Gallup #1–#5 instead.
- **Bright Horizons Modern Family Index summer numbers (87%, 76%, 60% PTO, 68% "break for everyone but me", 79%, 33%/33%)** — real publisher, real recurring index, but **no n, no fielding window and no pollster disclosed on any page I could retrieve** (businesswire and the investor site both blocked direct access; benefitnews.com reproduces the numbers and explicitly carries no methodology). Per the brief's "no number without publisher, year, and method" rule these are method-deficient. Directionally consistent with BPC #10 and LendingTree #15, so probably sound, but cite with the caveat.
- **"Children have around 50 days off from school (not including summer) in an average 180-day school year."** Appears in 2025 trade/opinion writing on school-calendar mismatch. No origin publisher found. Arithmetically plausible (365 − 104 weekend days − 180 school days ≈ 81 weekday non-school days, of which summer is ~50–60, leaving ~20–30 non-summer weekday closures), which suggests the circulating "50" is **too high** if it means weekdays. **Flagged as unverifiable and probably wrong.**
- **"More than 30 percent of U.S. parents secure structured summer childcare before the prior calendar year ends, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2023)."** Surfaced in camp-vendor content marketing. **BLS does not publish this.** This looks like a laundered restatement of CAP's 32%-start-before-March figure (#21) with a false BLS attribution bolted on. **Fabricated attribution — kill on sight.**
- **"A 2023 study found unexpected school closures forced mothers to cut six hours per week on average (72 hours over three months)."** Cited without a named study in a 2025 trade piece. Could not trace to an origin. Plausible and interesting if real; unusable as cited.
- **"Sawyer processed a record 13 million camp and activity bookings in 2025"** — vendor platform figure, quoted in an ACA sponsored post I could not retrieve. No method, no denominator. Vendor-interested.
- **UK data encountered and excluded**: 40% of parents expecting to take unpaid leave for summer childcare gaps; 52% lacking sufficient annual leave; 1-in-3 mothers cutting hours over the holidays. **These are UK surveys (HRreview / MadeForMums) and must not be presented as US.** No equivalent US measurement of unpaid-leave-for-summer was found — the closest US analogues are CAP #20 (2019, MTurk) and BPC #10 (2026, general care access, not summer-specific).
- **Spreadsheet/email/text tooling prevalence** — no sourced measurement found anywhere. If synthesis needs this, it must come from primary customer interviews, not from literature.
- **Gen Z parent camp behavior** — no data found, anywhere, at any tier. Expected per the brief. The structural argument (their kids are too young for camp) is inference, not measurement.
