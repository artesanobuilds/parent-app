# P8 — INTERACTION MODALITY: voice vs. typing

Collector P8. Compiled 2026-08-21. US focus. Brief rules applied: Tier D never citable; every number
carries publisher, year, method.

**Scope note.** This domain is the most polluted in the program. The first page of results for almost
every query is SEO aggregator content (sqmagazine.co.uk, serpwatch.io, keywordseverywhere, yaguara,
bigsur.ai, searchendurance, secondtalent, instantpress) recycling the same dozen numbers with no
publisher, no n, and no date. Roughly two-thirds of my working time went into tracing those to origin.
Several have no origin. See the dedicated section at the bottom.

**A methodological warning that runs through everything below.** Almost no public research separates
the three behaviors the brief asks about. "Voice assistant use" in Edison, Pew and eMarketer is a
single bucket that silently merges dictation, Siri/Alexa commands, and (recently) LLM voice modes.
Where I present a number for one of the three, I say so explicitly; where the source merges them, I
say that too. **The clean three-way split does not exist in published survey research as of
2026-08-21. That absence is itself a finding** — it means no one can hand you a defensible number for
"share of US parents who would hold a spoken conversation with an AI planner," and any vendor who
offers you one is extrapolating.

### NO DATA FOUND — explicit negative findings

These are real answers, not gaps I failed to fill. Each was searched to the limit of the session's
search budget and produced only Tier-D material.

- **Dictation/speech-to-text usage rate in the US.** There is no Tier A/B/C survey isolating "share of
  US adults who use speech-to-text into a keyboard, and how often." Every number in circulation
  ("57% use voice commands daily," "20.5% use voice search") is aggregator-only. The category most
  central to this brief is the one with the least real measurement.
- **Voice-assistant adoption by GENDER in the US, 2024–2026.** Pew's 2026 report breaks smart devices
  by age but I found no published gender crosstab for voice/smart-speaker use. Edison's Infinite Dial
  2026 deck does not break smart speakers by gender.
- **Parents using voice while physically managing children.** The only source is the NPR/Edison 2017
  and 2019 reports, and even they only publish that child-households use devices "more" — never a
  percentage. There is no time-use or diary research (ATUS carries no modality variable) connecting
  hands-occupied childcare to voice input.
- **Gen Z PARENTS (18–29 with children) and voice or AI-voice specifically.** No source disaggregates
  parents from non-parents within the 18–29 band on any modality question. As the brief predicted,
  this is the expected answer.
- **Session lengths for conversational LLM voice.** Neither OpenAI's NBER paper (Chatterji et al.,
  *How People Use ChatGPT*, w34255) nor Google publishes voice-mode session duration or task mix.
  OpenAI's paper classifies conversations by topic, not by input modality.
- **Any US survey measuring the share of people who avoid voice in public.** The strongest evidence is
  the German n=860 SEM study (#19–20), which measures continuance intention, not avoidance share.

### Interpretation

**1. The generational story is the opposite of the intuitive one, and it points at our exact user.**
Every modality-adjacent metric peaks or near-peaks in the 30–49 band — the millennial-parent cohort.
Smart-speaker ownership is highest there (41%, vs 33% for 18–29, Pew 2026). Daily AI chatbot use is
highest there (34%, vs 31%). This is the household-formation effect: smart speakers land in houses
with kitchens, kids and hands full, not in dorm rooms. Our core cohort is therefore the most
voice-equipped and the most AI-habituated group in America. But note the confidence gap: only 23% of
30–49s are extremely/very confident using chatbots vs 31% of 18–29s. They use it more and trust
themselves with it less. That combination argues for voice features that are forgiving and
low-stakes, not for a modality that is the only way to accomplish something.

**2. Voice-assistant engagement is real but SHALLOW, and that is the single most decision-relevant
number in this report.** Smart-speaker owners issue 12.4 requests per week (Edison/NPR 2022) — under
two per day, and Amazon's own internal read is that the majority have always been music, lights and
timers. Sixty-two percent of US adults use a voice assistant on some device, but the depth of that
use is a handful of two-second commands. There is no evidence anywhere in the literature that a US
consumer population has ever adopted voice for a multi-step, high-stakes, comparison-heavy task.
Alexa's >$25B loss is the market's verdict on trying: voice commerce failed precisely because product
discovery and comparison — which is *structurally what our Planner is* — cannot be done well by ear.

**3. Dictation and conversation are separable, and the evidence favors dictation.** Ruan et al. give
speech a 3.0x speed advantage AND a lower error rate — but only for TRANSCRIPTION, where the user
already knows the exact words. The same paper's literature review shows the advantage collapsing and
inverting for COMPOSITION (7.8 WPM speech vs 32.5 keyboard), because, as Shneiderman established, the
cognitive resources used to produce speech are the same ones used to solve problems. Speaking while
thinking is expensive. The clean implication: **voice wins when the user is emptying a formed thought
("swim lessons Tuesday at four"), and loses when the user is forming one ("which of these three camps
should I pick").** Ruan also found users preferred the KEYBOARD for correcting speech errors —
meaning the right architecture is voice-in / text-out-and-editable, never voice-only.

**4. Our product has the wrong half.** Per the brief's code audit, MVP ships (c) conversational
full-duplex ElevenLabs calls in two places and offers (a) dictation nowhere — no SFSpeechRecognizer,
and the mic was actively REMOVED from the Coach composer. Set that against the evidence: the
behavior with a 3x measured speed advantage, ubiquitous OS-level familiarity, near-zero social cost,
and a perfect fit for the app's 58 text-entry fields is the one we do not offer. The behavior with no
US adoption benchmark, an unmeasured session cost, a public-use shame penalty, and a documented
$25B failure precedent at task depth is the only one we do offer. This is close to an inversion of
what the literature supports.

**5. The barriers are context, not technology.** Privacy has been a stable ~27% objection since 2017
and ~55–63% object to the always-on microphone (Edison 2019) — but the larger barrier for non-users
is plain indifference (61%, Pew). For USERS, the binding constraint is social context: Distel et
al. (n=860, Germany) find continuance intention significantly lower in public in every scenario, with
shame the specific mediator in public and guilt in private. That is directly load-bearing for a
parenting app. A parent's planning moments are overwhelmingly semi-public or co-present — school
pickup line, a kid in the room, a partner on the couch, the pediatrician's waiting room. A
full-duplex spoken call about your child's camp registration, your budget, or your custody schedule
is exactly the content people will not speak aloud in company. Short dictation ("add: Maya soccer
Saturday 9am") survives that context; a two-minute conversation does not. **Caveat: the SEM study is
German, not US, and measures intention, not behavior. I found no US equivalent.**

**6. LLM voice may be changing the ceiling, but the evidence is vendor-grade.** Google's claim that
63% of Gemini users "now use voice" is the strongest signal that conversational voice has broken past
the timers-and-music plateau — and it is a company blog post with no n, no window, and no definition
of a voice user, published the same week as a milestone PR announcement. Search Engine Journal itself
flags this. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a fact. The countervailing number in the same disclosure is
more informative: ~1 in 5 Gemini Live interactions already need a camera or screen share. Even
Google's flagship voice product routinely needs a screen — which is what our Planner's fit-scored
candidate lists, conflict detection and lifecycle board are.

**7. Contradictions between sources, stated plainly.** (a) Smart-speaker ownership: Edison says 39% of
12+ (Jan 2026), Pew says 35% of 18+ (Feb 2026). Both are defensible; the gap is mostly the 12–17
band Edison includes and a question-wording difference (Pew asks specifically about a smart speaker
"with AI"). Do not present them as a trend. (b) Edison's own series shows 35% (2023) → 34% (2024) →
35% (2025) → 39% (2026); the 2024 dip coincides with Infinite Dial's migration off RDD telephone
sampling to a mixed online panel design, so the pre-2023 and post-2023 points are not strictly
comparable. Flag this if anyone builds a growth narrative on the 39%. (c) The aggregator claim that
"71% prefer voice over typing" is flatly contradicted by the only Pew measurement of that construct:
just 22% of voice-assistant users cite "it feels more natural than talking to a device by typing" as
a major reason for use, against 55% citing hands-free.

**8. What I would actually measure.** Because the published research cannot answer "will our parents
talk to this," the cheapest defensible substitute is our own instrumentation: session-start rate on
the two ElevenLabs entry points, median call duration, and abandonment inside the first 15 seconds —
segmented by time of day. If voice calls cluster into a narrow commute window and die everywhere else,
that replicates the hands-free finding (Pew: 55%) and tells you voice here is a car feature, not a
planning feature.

**The pattern to teach the rest of the program:** in this domain the laundering mechanism is almost
always one of four moves — (1) a FORECAST is restated as a MEASUREMENT, (2) a DEVICE count is restated

methodologised Edison figure from spring 2022 is now printed on a dozen "2026 statistics" pages.
