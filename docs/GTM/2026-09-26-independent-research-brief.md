## 5. Decision questions to answer

### A. What is the job, and who urgently wants it?

First conduct a bounded scan of major unmet needs relevant to practical support, wellbeing, agency, relationships, and daily functioning. Do not restrict it to affluent self-improvement customers or people already interested in AI. Consider several contrasting populations; possible starting points include unpaid caregivers, people navigating major life transitions, socially isolated adults, and people struggling to access or implement appropriate support. These are research prompts, not claims about which group has the greatest need.

For each promising need distinguish:

- **Burden:** severity, duration, frequency, and consequences for the person and others; cite appropriate population evidence without confusing prevalence with individual harm.
- **Underservice:** what help already exists, who cannot access it, why it falls short, and whether the gap is informational, relational, practical, financial, institutional, or clinical.
- **Potential benefit:** the specific improvement this service could plausibly cause beyond current alternatives, with uncertainty and risks made explicit.
- **Ability to help:** whether AI is useful, what human or professional involvement is necessary, and whether the intervention addresses a cause, a consequence, or neither. Do not present coaching as a fix for structural deprivation.
- **Reach and inclusion:** how people would discover, trust, and use the service, including accessibility, language, technology access, and privacy constraints.
- **Sustainability:** who benefits, who pays, and whether direct payment, a partner, an institution, or subsidy could sustain delivery. Low ability to pay does not mean low need.
- **Founder fit:** Miguel's credibility, motivation, capabilities, time, and access to suitable partners. These should inform feasibility without determining the winner in advance.

Avoid an artificial single-number ranking of society's suffering. Explain tradeoffs and uncertainty, then shortlist where meaningful unmet need intersects with credible benefit and feasible delivery. Carry at least two substantive alternatives outside the dad-focused concept into the final comparison. Explicitly state what evidence would justify walking away from dads, and what evidence would justify choosing dads despite a larger societal burden elsewhere.

For each direction identify:

- A specific adult buyer, a triggering moment, and the job they currently perform.
- A concrete successful outcome and how long it takes to observe.
- Current alternatives, including doing nothing, a human relationship, a general assistant, reminders, journaling, or an existing paid program.
- Why they would switch, what they would stop paying for, and why they would return.
- Whether value is episodic, seasonal, weekly, or continuous. Do not force subscription economics onto a naturally completed program.
- The minimum context and permissions needed to deliver value, and the work still done by the customer.

At minimum compare family logistics, difficult-parenting-moment support, implementing a personal practice, and career/leadership follow-through. Recovery support should be assessed separately, with appropriate expertise and burden, not treated as one more prompt template.

### B. What beats a general agent?

Examine actual current offerings of Muse and Grok Bot. Separate consumer availability, geographic restrictions, paid tiers, demonstrated features, and marketing promises. Include other relevant general assistants if they change the decision; date all capability claims.

Test the hypothesis that a complete packaged method is valuable to people who will not configure agents themselves. Conversely, test whether the same benefit can be achieved in 15 minutes with a good prompt and existing tools.

Use the same realistic tasks, context, follow-up interval, and allowed tools across comparisons. Avoid a “specialist with rich memory versus generic chatbot with none” strawman. If direct testing is unavailable, label it a desk comparison and leave reliability unscored.

Ask what remains differentiating after model quality, memory, voice, and browsing improve further. Possible answers include distribution, trusted method, longitudinal outcome data with consent, a specialized workflow, or a human relationship. Do not call any of these a moat without evidence.

### C. Are visible specialists helpful?

Distinguish three independent choices:

1. One versus multiple underlying agents.
2. One versus multiple visible identities.
3. One versus multiple customer jobs.

Assess routing errors, contradictory advice, memory leakage, latency, cost, user confusion, and accountability. Test a single coherent coach with internal tools against visible specialists. Require evidence that specialist presentation improves outcomes or preference enough to offset complexity.

A named character should be clearly AI, not impersonate a clinician or real teacher, and not create false expectations of constant human care. The relevant goal is user agency and real-world progress, not maximizing emotional dependence or conversation length.
